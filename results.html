RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Observed Coral Cover Pattern

Using the cleaned AIMS hard coral dataset, mean coral cover was calculated across years to establish a baseline for coral dynamics on the Great Barrier Reef. The resulting time series shows substantial variability. This reflects known disturbance and recovery processes affecting reef systems such as bleaching, overfishing, and human interferance. This observed variability motivates the use of a dynamic modeling framework rather than assuming coral cover is constant through time.

Spatial Variation In Microplastics

Marine microplastics data reveal a strong spatial variability in concentration. After filtering and scaling, regional mean microplastic levels differ across the Northern, Central, and Southern GBR. These differences are carried forward into the model as region-specific initial conditions, ensuring that each region’s dynamics reflect its observed pollution.

Dynamics Without Allee Effect

In the absence of an Allee effect, coral growth follows a modified logistic trajectory in which recovery is slowed, by microplastic stress. The logistic component allows coral cover to increase when population size is low, while the microplastic inhibition term reduces growth in proportion to both coral cover and microplastic concentration. As a result, coral cover does not collapse outright under a constant microplastic input rate, but instead stabilizes at a reduced equilibrium below the carrying capacity.

Microplastic dynamics in this model reveal important feedbacks between coral health and pollution. Although the input rate of microplastics is identical across all regions, microplastic concentrations diverge over time. This is due to how coral-dependent removal varies regionally. Regions that start with higher coral cover maintain a stronger removal capacity. This allows microplastic concentrations to decline despite continued input. On the other hand, regions with lower coral cover remove microplastics more slowly, thus leading to accumulation. Biologically, this suggests that relatively healthy reefs may partially buffer themselves against pollution, while already degraded reefs experience increasing stress.

Dynamics With Allee Effect

When an Allee effect is included, coral population dynamics become much more fragile. The Allee term reduces coral growth at low population sizes, representing biological processes such as reduced reproductive success. Under these conditions, coral populations starting closer to the Allee threshold recover more slowly and may fail to recover altogether.

This increased vulnerability strongly affects microplastic dynamics. In regions where coral cover declines toward or below the Allee threshold, microplastic removal weakens dramatically because the coral population can no longer trap or retain particles efficiently. Consequently, microplastic concentrations increase even though the external input rate remains constant. In contrast, regions with sufficiently high coral cover avoid crossing the threshold, retain removal capacity, and continue to reduce microplastic concentrations over time. These outcomes illustrate how ecological thresholds can transform gradual environmental stress into abrupt, region-specific degradation.

Discussion

The varied trends across areas mostly stem from how much coral was there at first and the amount of microplastics present, both pulled straight from actual measurements. Although each region gets the same level of plastic input, outcomes differ. Biologically, damaged reefs tend to weaken further over time. When there’s little coral left, regrowth becomes harder while constant pollution stays a bigger threat. Yet areas rich in coral handle pressure better since strong colonies filter out tiny plastics more effectively.

This project reveals how corals and microplastics mix differently depending on location, even if pollution levels are identical. Real-world data at the start shows how coral growth and plastic cleanup influence each other strongly over time. If you add an Allee effect, areas with less coral struggle harder because dropping too low lowers the ability for the coral to bounce back and lets pollutants pile up faster.

These findings stress the urgency of reducing microplastic accumulation in Australian coral reefs. Without intervention systems may be pushed to collapse faster than anticipated leading to many global food crises, ecosystem death and loss in financial stability within those regions.

Future Work

The major plan for future work revolves around the idea of higher accuracy. To do so, the project will no longer be able to focus on the effect that only microplastics have on the coral reefs. Future work will need to involve other factors such as bleaching events, natural death, and further human impacts on the biomass of the coral reefs. To implement these changes into the models, new parameters and variables will be added to our ordinary differential equations. These new parameters will be able to encapsulate more of the impact humans and natural disasters have on the coral biomass and will help future generations understand what measures can be taken to help reduce detrimental effects and encourage more stable conditions for the recovery of the coral. In addition, more models will need to be developed to display the lone impacts of bleaching, natural disasters, and human effects. These models will be similar to the time and spatial visualization models. Another thought for future work is to create some sort of simulation that may help reduce the noise of samples taken just below the surface. This is a very aspirational goal and one that will require a significant amount of trial and error, and it will likely still face around the same amount of error as the current model from samples taken. Finally, we would love to see increased testing and information surrounding microplastic impact on the coral growth rates so models can be finely tuned and optimized.
